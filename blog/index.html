How to Pay Off a Mortgage Faster (Real Numbers on a $400k Loan)

KN
By Kelon Nyanguno
August 16, 2026 9 min read

"Pay off my mortgage faster" usually means one of a few different things: a shorter loan term, extra payments on a standard term, or switching to biweekly payments. Each one changes your monthly payment and total interest very differently. Below are the actual numbers on a $400,000 loan at today's average 30-year fixed rate (6.75%, illustrative — your actual rate depends on your lender, credit, and down payment), so you can see which approach fits your budget before you commit to one.

Before the numbers: this article is for general education, not personalized financial advice. Run your own numbers with our free house affordability calculator, and talk to a mortgage advisor before restructuring a real loan.

The Real Cost of Different Payoff Timelines

Here's what a $400,000 loan actually costs at different terms, assuming a 6.75% fixed rate:

TermMonthly PaymentTotal Interest PaidTotal Paid

25 years$2,764$429,094$829,094

The pattern is consistent: cutting your term in half roughly doubles your monthly payment but cuts total interest by more than half. That trade-off — higher payment now versus far less total cost — is the entire decision behind "paying off a mortgage faster."

Can You Pay Off a $400k Mortgage in 5 Years?

Mathematically, yes — but the monthly payment required is $7,873. That's roughly three times the standard 30-year payment, and it assumes no other major debt or expenses competing for that cash. For most households, this only becomes realistic through a combination of a high income, a large inheritance or bonus applied as a lump sum, or downsizing to a smaller loan altogether. If a 5-year payoff is the goal, it's usually more achievable by starting with a smaller loan amount than by trying to out-earn a $400k balance on a compressed timeline.

How Much Faster Do Biweekly Payments Actually Help?

Biweekly payments work by splitting your monthly payment in half and paying that half every two weeks. Since there are 52 weeks in a year, that works out to 26 half-payments — the equivalent of 13 full monthly payments instead of 12. You're making one extra payment a year without really feeling it.

On a $400,000 30-year loan at 6.75%, switching to biweekly payments pays off the loan in about 24 years instead of 30, and saves roughly $126,000 in interest — without changing your monthly budget in any noticeable way.

The catch: not all lenders apply biweekly payments correctly. Some just hold the extra half-payment until it accumulates into a full payment (no benefit), while others apply it directly to principal as it arrives (full benefit). Confirm with your servicer how the extra payment is applied before assuming you're getting the full effect — or replicate it manually by simply adding 1/12th of your monthly payment as extra principal each month, which produces the same result and guarantees it's applied correctly.

What Extra Principal Payments Actually Save You

If biweekly isn't an option, adding a fixed extra amount to your monthly principal has a similar effect. Here's the impact on the same $400,000, 30-year, 6.75% loan:

Extra Monthly PaymentNew Payoff TimeTotal Interest PaidInterest Saved
+$0 (baseline)30.0 years$533,981—
+$200/month24.3 years$414,869$119,112
+$500/month19.3 years$316,459$217,522
+$1,000/month14.7 years$230,185$303,796

Notice that the first few hundred dollars of extra payment does most of the heavy lifting — the jump from $0 to $500 extra saves more per dollar than going from $500 to $1,000. That's because you're attacking principal earliest, while the loan balance (and the interest it generates) is at its highest.

Pro tip: before committing to extra mortgage payments, confirm your lender doesn't charge a prepayment penalty, and check whether you'd come out ahead investing that money instead — if your mortgage rate is below what you could reasonably earn investing, paying down high-interest debt or investing may beat extra mortgage payments.

At What Age Do Most People Actually Pay Off Their Mortgage?

Later than most people expect. Recent Census Bureau data shows roughly 63% of homeowners aged 65 and over own their home free and clear, compared with under 28% of working-age homeowners under 65. In other words, for most people, "paid off" clusters around or after retirement age rather than in their 40s or 50s — even though that's when many people assume it happens. If your goal is a payoff well before that, it takes a deliberate acceleration strategy like the ones above, not just making minimum payments and waiting.

A Simple Way to Decide Your Approach

Work through these in order:

1. Can you comfortably afford a shorter fixed term (15 or 20 years) at today's rates? If yes, this is usually the cleanest option — lower total interest, and the lender enforces the discipline for you.

2. If not, can you commit to a fixed extra amount every month without straining your budget? Even $200/month, sustained, saves six figures in interest over the loan's life.

3. If extra payments feel unpredictable, switch to biweekly instead. It forces the "extra payment" through payment frequency rather than willpower, which works better for most people psychologically.

4. Before any of the above, confirm you have a full emergency fund. A paid-off mortgage doesn't help if a $3,000 repair or a lost paycheck forces you into high-interest debt because your cash was all going toward principal.
